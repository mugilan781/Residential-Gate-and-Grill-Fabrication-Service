Classical vs. modern gates

Which one will your house thank you for in 20 years? Our data says the answer surprises everyone.

DesignMay 20269 min readBy Ravi Iyer

Which gate will your house thank you for in 20 years?

We've built 500 residential gates and renovated 40 more. That second number is the one that matters — renovating an old gate is the fastest way to learn what actually survives.

Every few months, a homeowner calls us about a gate that was "brand new" five years ago. The paint has blistered, the welds have gone brown at the joints, or the motor sounds like a blender. And every few months, we get the same question: should we have gone classical or modern?

The honest answer, after 500 installs and 40 renovations, is that the style is almost never the problem. The problem is the stuff you can't see in the brochure: the steel grade, the weld preparation, the finish system and the automation design. A boring classical gate built honestly will outlast a glamorous modern gate built cheaply, every single time.

What actually fails first

Let's talk about the renovation log, because it's the closest thing we have to autopsies for gates. Of the 40 gates we've rebuilt or repaired:

  • 31 had finish failure — blisters, flaking and rust blooms — traced to site-painted or un-phosphated steel.
  • 27 had weld corrosion at the joints, where "touch-up paint" had never reached.
  • 19 had motors undersized for the actual gate weight, usually because the gate was built without a weight budget.
  • 12 had door frames plumb by eye, which is to say, not plumb at all.

Notice what's missing from that list: style. Nobody ever called us to say their scrollwork was "too old-fashioned." Nobody complained that their horizontal slats looked dated. What they complained about was rust, sagging and motors that die.

Style is what you see on day one. The grade, the prep and the finish are what you see on day 2,000.
— Ravi Iyer, Founder, IRONHAUS

Classical: the renovation advantage

Classical gates earn their keep on the repair bench. A damaged scroll can be re-rolled, a broken bar can be spliced, and a full repaint is a weekend job. The geometry is forgiving — ornamental steel hides the small movements that modern designs expose instantly.

There's also a financial pattern worth naming: classical gates tend to hold their value on resale in the older neighbourhoods we work in, because buyers read them as permanence. In a street of stone-pillared entrances, a modern frameless gate looks like a decision; a classical gate looks like a fact.

Modern: the design advantage

Modern gates win on daylight, sightlines and proportion. A horizontal-slat or frameless design makes a narrow entrance feel wider and a low boundary feel taller. They photograph beautifully, which matters more than most fabricators admit.

But modern design has zero forgiveness. A 6-metre cantilever slide with slats has to be flat to the millimetre, or the gap looks like a mistake. The finish has to be flawless, because there's no ornament to distract the eye. This is why modern gates punish cheap fabrication faster than classical ones do — the same shortcuts show up in a month, not a year.

What we'd actually build

If you asked us to design one gate for a 30-year horizon, it would look like this: a modern-proportioned frame with a single classical accent — one hand-rolled scroll at the hinge post, or a leaf edge that mirrors the house's arch. It's a combination that reads as timeless precisely because it refuses to pick a lane.

And beneath that style, it would be boring in all the right ways: hot-dip galvanised substrate where coastal, zinc-phosphated and powder-coated everywhere, automation sized at 1.5× the calculated load, and a structural warranty in writing.

That's the part the brochure can't fake. And it's the part you'll thank us for at year 20.
